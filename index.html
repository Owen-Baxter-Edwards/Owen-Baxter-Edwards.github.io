Generational Credit Card Delinquency Analysis

PythonstatsmodelsFirst-Differenced OLSFRED Data
Context

Credit card delinquency has climbed since its pandemic-era lows, and Federal Reserve data shows Generation Z carrying the highest average delinquency rate of any generation. The open question for lenders and policymakers is whether that gap comes down to financial inexperience or whether younger borrowers are simply more exposed to the same macroeconomic swings everyone faces.

My Role

I independently sourced ten years of quarterly data from the Federal Reserve Banks of New York and St. Louis, first-differenced every series to address non-stationarity, and built four separate regressions (one per generation) of delinquency-rate changes on unemployment, inflation, interest rates, and real disposable income. I then worked through why two of the coefficients came back with the opposite sign from what theory predicts instead of dismissing it as noise.

Outcome
Found that unemployment and inflation were statistically significant predictors of delinquency for every generation, real disposable income was significant for three of the four (all but Baby Boomers), and interest rates were not significant for any of them. The size of each effect grew steadily with generation. Generation Z's delinquency rate moved almost 3 times more per point of unemployment change than Baby Boomers' did (coefficients of -0.136 versus -0.046), and Generation Z also carried the highest average delinquency rate of the four generations at 2.66 percent, compared to 0.96 percent for Baby Boomers. Both the unemployment and inflation coefficients came back negative, the opposite sign standard economic theory predicts. I traced that reversal to pandemic-era stimulus checks and loan forbearance, which temporarily decoupled missed payments from labor-market stress, a pattern consistent with research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia.

Hurricane Helene Twitter Informativeness Classification

PythonCNN / NLPMatplotlibPandas
Context

During Hurricane Helene, emergency responders faced an overwhelming, unfiltered stream of social media posts, making it hard to separate actionable information from noise in real time — a version of the "data volume without data quality" problem that now ranks among the top blockers to data-driven decision-making across industries.

My Role

As one of 3 researchers in the CADS Lab, I built and trained a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) text classifier to label tweets as informative or noninformative, engineered comparative visualizations in Python (Matplotlib, Pandas) across the disaster's response and recovery phases, and co-authored the final research paper.

Outcome
Quantified a 20 percentage-point drop in informative tweet share between response and recovery phases — a concrete, presentable signal disaster-response teams can use to recalibrate social-listening effort as a crisis unfolds.

AI Adoption's Effect on Industry Employment and In-Demand Skills

PythonDifference-in-DifferencespandasBLS & O*NET Data
Context

As AI tools spread through the workplace, employers, job seekers, and policymakers all want the same two answers. Which industries are actually losing or gaining jobs because of it, and which technical skills are worth prioritizing. Getting a reliable answer means pulling multi-year government labor data and running it through a research design that can separate an AI-driven shift from ordinary economic noise.

My Role

As project lead of an eight-person research team in NC State's Data Analytics Club, I built the full analytical pipeline behind the project. I cleaned and merged four years of Bureau of Labor Statistics data across five NAICS industries into a single panel dataset of employment, wages, and establishment counts, and separately normalized over 60,000 raw O*NET technology-skill entries with regex-based text cleaning so demand trends could be tracked reliably from 2022 to 2025. From there I built the difference-in-differences regression treating the most AI-exposed industry as the treatment group with industry and year fixed effects, along with the demand-rate calculations behind the skills findings. Teammates helped shape the research questions, source supporting data, and present sections of the findings, but I wrote the code and ran the analysis end to end.

Outcome
The chart on the left tells most of the story. The most AI-exposed industry (Information, NAICS 51) went from employment growth of about plus 8.7 percent in 2022 to averaging about negative 1.2 percent across 2023 to 2025, a roughly 10-point swing. The other four industries only slid about 2 points on average over that same stretch (from roughly plus 3.9 percent to plus 2.0 percent), so a difference-in-differences model confirmed that industry 51's decline was about 8 percentage points steeper than what the other industries experienced (p < .001, controlling for industry and year fixed effects). Wages in that industry kept rising over the same period, a pattern consistent with automation of lower-level roles paired with rising pay for the higher-skill workers who remain, a trend corroborated by outside labor-market data from Lightcast and the World Economic Forum that the team cited to stress-test the findings.
